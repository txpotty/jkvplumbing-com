    Frequently Asked Questions About Grease Trap Installation in Cheyenne

    Expect roughly $1,500 to $4,000 for a small indoor trap and $7,000 to $20,000 or more for an outdoor gravity interceptor. A floor-recessed trap costs more than one set on the floor. Location drives the rest. A tank under a parking lot needs a traffic-rated lid and asphalt repair, which a tank in a grass strip does not.

    As close to the grease-producing fixtures as the space allows, so grease does not cool and coat the pipe before it reaches the trap. Leave room to open the lid and clean it without moving equipment. Never under a cook line. Outdoor interceptors go where a pump truck can park within reach of the hose and where the lids will not end up under a dumpster or a parked car.

    The common rule is to clean before grease and solids fill 25 percent of the trap's depth. For a small indoor trap in a busy kitchen, that can be weekly. Outdoor tanks go longer. Many gravity interceptors are pumped every 30 to 90 days, and your local FOG program may set a maximum interval no matter what the trap looks like.

    Two warranties apply here. The trap body carries the maker's coverage, which runs from one year on some steel units to much longer on plastic. Our labor warranty covers the joints and the setting, and you get it in writing up front.

    We start with a fixture list and the drain rate of each fixture. Each sink's volume and drain time give a flow rate, and the dish machine's spec sheet gives its flow. That gives the GPM figure. Hydromechanical traps are picked by that GPM rating, while gravity interceptors are sized by volume and retention time from the same data.

    Plan on a permit. Both the building department and the sewer authority usually have a say. You get copies of every approval for your files. Unpermitted traps cause trouble at lease transfer.

    It keeps cooking grease out of the public sewer. Inside the trap, warm wastewater slows and cools, so grease rises and stays put while water flows on. Grease that reaches the mains hardens into blockages, and the sewer authority bills the kitchens it traces back to. Your own drain lines benefit too.

    Watch the pump-out bills. Rising service frequency means the trap has been outgrown or the baffles have failed. Smell is another clue. Slow floor sinks, grease odor at the trap, a lid that no longer seals, or a failed FOG inspection all point the same way, and we can tell you which it is in one visit.
