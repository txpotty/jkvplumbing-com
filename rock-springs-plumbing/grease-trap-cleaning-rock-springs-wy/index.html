    Frequently Asked Questions About Grease Trap Cleaning in Rock Springs

    Small under-sink traps often start around $200 a visit. Large outdoor interceptors can pass $1,000 when they are full and the cap has hardened. Disposal is billed by the gallon at the receiving facility, so the gallons hauled drive most of the cost, and a 1,500-gallon tank fills a truck.

    It is the most common pumping standard. Pump before grease and solids fill a quarter of the depth. Past that point the water moves through too fast for grease to separate, so grease rides out the outlet even though the trap looks like it is working.

    Short term, slow drains and smell. Longer term, a hardened grease cap that can take hours to break up. Hydrogen sulfide from the rotting grease also attacks concrete, so interceptors that go unpumped for years often need new baffles and risers well before their time.

    It depends on trap size and menu. Commercial kitchens usually land between every two weeks and every three months. A home with a small under-sink unit might only need it once a year, while a busy fryer kitchen with a 50-gallon trap can need it every other week.

    Slow sinks come first. Gurgling floor drains and a sour smell by the dish station usually follow. Gray water rising through a floor sink means the trap is far past 25 percent and grease is already moving downstream.

    The terms get mixed up constantly. Most people use trap for the small indoor unit, usually under 100 gallons. An interceptor is the big in-ground tank outside, often 500 gallons or more, and plumbing codes treat them as separate categories with different sizing rules.

    Zurn, Schier, Watts and Big Dipper units turn up across Wyoming every week, along with Rockford traps and older steel boxes from makers that are long gone. We can usually find parts. On discontinued models, a universal baffle or flow control often fits, and if the steel has rusted through we quote a replacement.

    No permit is needed just to have a trap pumped. The records are your responsibility. Installing a new trap or interceptor, or changing the size of one, usually needs a plumbing permit and sometimes a pretreatment program review before the work starts.
