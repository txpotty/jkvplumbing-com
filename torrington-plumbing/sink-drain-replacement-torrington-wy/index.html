    Look at the bottom of the trap. Rust spots, white crust around the slip nuts, or a damp ring on the cabinet floor under the J-bend all mean the drain is leaking or close to it, even if you have not seen a drip yet. Press on the trap with your hand. Soft spots mean it is done.

    Usually the trap has not refilled or a joint is loose. A new trap needs water run through it to form its seal, and a slip joint that is slightly off can let gas past, so run the faucet for a minute and check the connections for dampness. We fix it free under warranty.

    On a newer drain, just the part. On a drain more than fifteen years old, especially a chrome or thin brass one, the other joints are usually close behind, and replacing the full assembly once costs less than several service calls. We price both for you.

    Odor usually means the trap seal is weak. A trap that leaks slowly, a sink that is rarely used and dries out, or a missing vent that siphons the water out of the trap will all let sewer gas into the room. Gunk on the stopper smells too.
