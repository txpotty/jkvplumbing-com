    How to Protect Your Pipes Before the Next Wyoming Freeze

    By Andrew Blake · October 6, 2026 · 5 min read

    Homeowners here rely on heat tape, pipe insulation and frost-free hose bibs to keep lines from freezing in garages and crawlspaces.

    Pipes do not need a long winter to burst. Plenty of places with mild weather still get a night or two each year below freezing for six hours or more. The houses that get hurt are the ones built as if it never freezes. Supply lines run in exterior walls, the backflow preventer sits bare in the yard, and the pool pump has no cover at all. One night like that can split a pipe that has been fine for twenty years.

    Why Frozen Pipes Burst

    Water expands about nine percent as it freezes. The ice plug itself is rarely what splits the pipe. When the plug forms, it shoves the water ahead of it toward a closed faucet or valve, and pressure builds in that trapped section until the copper splits or a brass valve body cracks. While the line is still frozen, the crack stays plugged with ice. So the leak shows up the next morning, when the sun hits the pipe and water starts spraying.

    Above-ground pipe is the weak spot in most houses. Bare copper and PVC lose heat fast on a clear night once the sun goes down. Wind across an open yard pulls heat away even faster. A backflow preventer sitting in the open on a windy night can freeze solid at temperatures that a pipe inside a wall would never notice.

    The Parts of a House Most at Risk

    Before the forecast drops, take a lap around the house with a flashlight and check these spots:

    • Backflow preventers on irrigation and fire lines. The brass body sits above ground and costs real money to replace when it splits.
    • Hose bibs on exterior walls, especially older models that are not frost-free.
    • Pool and spa equipment, meaning the pump, filter, heater and the PVC between them.
    • Irrigation risers and drip manifolds sitting out in the open in the planting beds.
    • Evaporative cooler supply lines that climb the outside wall to the roof, on houses that have them.
    • Water heaters and supply pipe in unheated garages, mostly in colder areas.
    • The main supply where it comes up out of the ground near the meter or pressure regulator.

    Cheap Steps That Prevent Damage

    Most freeze protection costs less than a service call. Slide foam pipe sleeves over every exposed run and tape the seams so cold air cannot get in. Pull the hose off each hose bib and fit an insulated cover, since a hose left attached holds water right at the valve seat. For a backflow preventer, use an insulated pouch, or wrap it in old towels and set a bucket over it. Where hard freezes show up every winter, a run of outdoor-rated heat tape on the worst stretch pays for itself the first night.

    Check the pool controller for a freeze protection setting. On most newer controllers it kicks the pump on once the air sensor reads a few degrees above freezing. During a hard freeze, let a faucet on an outside wall drip overnight, which relieves pressure and keeps water moving. Open the sink cabinets on exterior walls. Warm room air needs a path to the pipes.

    Cold, windy winters, dry air and big temperature swings are the main climate stresses on plumbing across Wyoming.

    Colder climates call for permanent fixes. Frost-free sillcocks put the valve seat well inside the heated wall. Insulated crawlspaces and heat tape on the runs that froze last year do most of the rest. If you leave a cabin or second home empty for the winter, shut off the main, open the low drains and blow the lines out, because a dry pipe has nothing in it to freeze.

    If a Pipe Freezes

    A trickle from the kitchen faucet on a cold morning usually means a frozen line. Leave that faucet open. Warm the suspect section slowly with a hair dryer or a space heater kept well back. Never use a torch or any open flame. Have the main shutoff located first, because a split pipe leaks the minute the ice lets go. A split hose bib or one section of pipe usually runs $200 to $500 to repair, and replacing a cracked backflow preventer runs roughly $400 to $900 installed, more for larger sizes.
