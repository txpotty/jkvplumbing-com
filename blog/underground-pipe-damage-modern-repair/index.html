    Underground Pipe Damage: Common Causes and Modern Repair Solutions

    By Luis Ortega · August 31, 2026 · 5 min read

    What Goes Wrong Under the Yard

    Your sewer lateral and water service run from the house to the street, usually somewhere 2 to 6 feet underground. They stay out of mind until the lawn gets soggy or a toilet backs up. Most laterals are 4-inch, and on older houses that usually means clay or cast iron. Each fails in its own way.

    Common Causes

    Tree Roots

    Roots find sewer lines by following moisture from a cracked joint or a loose gasket. A single fine root gets inside, then spreads into a mat that snags paper and grease. Clay pipe is the worst for this. The joints every two or three feet give roots a way in, and a mature oak or maple can fill a 4-inch line in a few seasons.

    Shifting Soil

    Basements and crawlspaces are both common in Wyoming homes, and both benefit from protection from freezing and groundwater.

    Soil that settles or swells moves the pipe with it. Rigid pipe like clay or cast iron does not bend, so it either cracks or separates at a joint. Sags are common too. A sag, which we call a belly, holds water and waste, and on camera it looks like a stretch that stays full after flushing.

    Old Pipe Material

    Housing in Wyoming includes older ranch homes, manufactured homes and newer builds, with plumbing ranging from galvanized pipe to PEX.

    Age wears every material down. Cast iron rusts from the inside, and the bottom of the pipe can corrode through while the top still looks solid. Orangeburg pipe, used through the early 1970s, is pressed tar paper that slowly flattens into an oval. Galvanized water service lines close up with rust and lose pressure at every faucet.

    Signs of a Buried Line Problem

    Several drains slowing at once points to the main line. A gurgling toilet when the washing machine drains is a classic sign. Outside, look for a patch of grass that stays greener than the rest, or soft ground over the path of the line, especially in dry weather. Sewer smell near the cleanout is another.

    On the water side, the meter tells the story. Turn off every fixture and watch the leak indicator, the small triangle or dial on the meter face. A spinning indicator means water is leaving somewhere between the meter and the house, and a buried service line is a likely spot. Low pressure throughout the house can mean the same.

    A sewer camera settles it. We run a camera through the cleanout and record the whole line, with a locator that marks depth and position on the ground above. You see the same video we do. The footage decides the repair.

    Modern Repair Options

    Trench repair is still the right call for some jobs. Collapsed pipe or a badly sagging run generally gets excavated, because a liner needs pipe that still holds its shape. Spot repairs can be small. A single broken joint under the yard might mean a 4 by 6 foot hole and a day of work.

    Trenchless lining avoids most of the digging. A felt liner soaked in epoxy resin is pulled into the line, inflated, and cured into a pipe within the pipe. Most residential liners go in through the cleanout and one small access pit, and the job takes a day. Roots cannot get through it.

    Pipe bursting is the other trenchless method. A cone-shaped head is winched through the old pipe, splitting it and pulling new HDPE pipe into place behind it. You need a pit on each end, but the lawn in between stays untouched. Our crews at JKV Plumbing use both methods across Wyoming, depending on what the camera shows.

    When to Call

    Do not wait on a sewer line that has backed up twice. Each backup leaves waste in the line, and roots grow faster with that steady food supply. Most of our camera inspections take under an hour, and you get a copy of the video plus a marked-up location of the problem. Reach JKV Plumbing at (307) 393-4049. Ask about the cleanout location when you call, since an accessible cleanout saves time.
